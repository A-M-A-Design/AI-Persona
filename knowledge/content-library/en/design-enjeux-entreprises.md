# How can design answer the challenges companies face today?
Article written by Arthur Mathon (LinkedIn). Tags: Business, DesignOps, OrgDesign, Research.

Companies today are striving to exist and sustain their activity in a world of constant acceleration. Every day, new forces disturb their equilibrium and redefine the rules of the game. Fluctuations in the price of resources and energy, often driven by ecological and political crises, put their stability under severe strain. The global economy, meanwhile, struggles to settle, which makes it harder for entrepreneurs to plan ahead. The lifespan of technologies, once a guarantee of longevity, is shortening drastically, and the pace of technological innovation gives companies no respite. The direct consequence is that the durability of their products, their services and their organisational systems is called into question. In short, a company performing well in 2024 is no longer assured of prospering in 2025. These challenges call for deeper reflection on the cult of performance and profitability, a pillar of our society since the emergence of capitalism. While the pursuit of efficiency and maximum productivity has long dominated, it is becoming necessary to re-examine those priorities in the face of today's challenges. Through this reflection, design emerges as a discipline capable of playing a fundamental role in meeting these contemporary challenges. By reinventing the scope of its action, I am convinced that design can offer durable answers to the problems companies confront.

## 1. Systems thinking to redefine the structure of organisations

A system is a set of elements that interact with one another. Systems are everywhere, in nature as in our society. Today, pseudo-systemic thinking dominates: it consists in trying to change the system by acting only on the quantity and quality of flows, without changing the network of interactions. Almost every organisational model in business, for instance, is modelled on the same template. We find production silos (engineering, design and so on), support departments (human resources, finance) and decision-making islands (leadership team, shareholders). In pseudo-systemic thinking the nature of the links remains unchanged; only the elements of the system are considered. Shareholders and executives still talk to each other to define a strategic and business vision. Designers and engineers collaborate with great difficulty to define products and services. Sales and marketing work to develop the company's offering and image.

By contrast, systems thinking consists in changing the system by modifying the interactions between the elements that compose it. You can therefore conceive of a society and an organisation made of the same elements — support, production, decision-making — but interacting in a singular way. In this inversion of paradigm, you question the nature of the collaboration between a shareholder and a factory worker, between an executive and a cleaner, between a salesperson and a designer, between a recruiter and an engineer. The Meadows report told us as much back in 1972: it is illusory to imagine a different outcome if the nature of the interactions is not modified. "Running the same system harder or faster will not change the trajectory as long as the structure of the system is not changed."1 Design has a part to play in bringing this transition about. As a human science, it takes a particular interest in interactions. That is why a great majority of the tools it uses embody systems thinking: user flows, user journeys, mind maps. It becomes clear, then, why most job descriptions call for empathy, tree-structured thinking or adaptability — essential skills in building coherent systems. More recently, one discipline has taken on new importance: systems design. I am convinced that design, by taking an interest in the structure of organisations — in other words, in the connections — can answer the challenges they face.

## 2. Redefining the nature of interactions to build trust

In an era obsessed with performance and control, the connection between individuals has become greatly impoverished, reduced to something mechanistic: the notorious process. It is then particularly difficult to conceive of intimacy, relationship and trust other than through a lens of control. Yet in any cooperation, real strength emerges when we make room for vulnerability, when we accept opening a space for doubt, for questioning, and above all for what we do not know. Olivier Hamant, biologist and researcher at INRA, put it well: "In a cooperation, you have to be able to expose your vulnerabilities. If you hide them, you are in the world of performance. In the world of robustness, you expose your vulnerabilities, and they stimulate interactions and strengthen the group."2 But how do we make this vision real in our professional practices, where the human being is stripped of substance, where intimacy is tolerated only within the sphere of Human Resources? You will readily notice the paradox born of pairing the words "Resources" and "Human". One concrete action we can take to embody this vision is to turn formulations of the type "I want" and "I know" into "I would like" and "I don't know". This feels counter-intuitive, especially for a manager or an executive who, in the collective imagination, must know where they are going and be in control of their company's design. That "I want" seems to embody a direction, an authority, a commitment. But what does it really imply? It presupposes that we already know the path, that we hold the answer, that the road is marked out. This kind of language closes the space, reduces the potential for exchange and discourages innovation. Why share your ideas if everything is already decided, if the point is to apply a ready-made recipe? Collaboration is impoverished, exchanges become exchanges of convenience, superficial interactions where nothing personal is risked. But what happens when an executive, a manager, a colleague dares to venture into "I would like" or "I don't know"? At first sight this might seem fragile, uncertain. How can we move forward if we do not know exactly where we are going? Yet it is precisely these expressions of vulnerability that open a field of authentic, living relationships. It is in this slight shift that a manager or executive can become a Leader. Saying "I would like" makes room for momentum without claiming to impose a certainty. It invites every member of the team to contribute, to enrich the initial vision, to make the project their own. "I don't know" is a call to dialogue, a door open to other people's ideas, a space where relationship and trust can genuinely emerge. These are words that create a bond of humanity and intimacy. Leadership then becomes a relational quality, a posture of openness and active listening, where trust is established through shared and sincere vulnerability. Imagine a team whose leader begins not by stating firm directives but by saying: "I would like us to explore together our company's business model, so we can face the transformations of our sector", or "I don't know exactly what the best solution is, but I would like us to look for it together." In this configuration the team is recognised not as mere executors of a predefined plan, but as partners in a collective adventure. Each person feels they can create impact, that they are welcomed in their own capacity to question, to doubt, to propose. Trust becomes common ground, founded on shared vulnerability. And that trust is active, nourished by a relational intimacy that can only be born in the acceptance of fragility.

## 3. A small shift for managers, a giant leap for team cohesion

The current trend, with corporate seminars handing out the "10 Commandments of Productivity", seems to move in the opposite direction to the momentum proposed above. All it does is relay the vision of a handful of shareholders who are not even from our profession, as if there were a recipe for success applicable to every situation and every individual, and without ever coming into contact with them. What is implied could not be clearer: follow the rule and you will reach the objective. But life, business and creation cannot be reduced to injunctions. I would go further: there is a certain violence in these injunctions. It is born of the principle of treating people as "Resources". How can we inspire genuine team cohesion if everyone is ordered to apply external rules, without ever risking an exploration of their own aspirations, their own doubts? It becomes clear that the leader's real role is to create a frame, a general direction, while letting the details and the means of getting there emerge from the group's interactions. The leader inspires and guides, but does not control. They act more like a gardener who prepares the ground, feeds ideas and lets talent flourish, than like a commander who dictates and imposes. This shift from "I want" to "I would like" is a subtle but profound revolution, moving from an authoritarian approach to an inspiring, inclusive one.

> It is not doubt, it is certainty that drives you mad. — Friedrich Nietzsche

The modern leader, then, is the one who can give up certainties, who dares to explore possibilities alongside their team, and who fosters a dynamic of exchange. This is a leader who, far from hiding behind commandments or fixed objectives, accompanies their team through uncertainty, recognising that an organisation's true power lies in the diversity and intimacy of human relationships. Performance is no longer an end in itself, but the natural outcome of this sincere and living cohesion. If we want to build teams, projects and organisations that are genuinely alive, we must move beyond the illusion of control. We must invite every member into the human adventure of work, with its uncertainties, its doubts and its failures. That is where leadership lies: in a leader's capacity to accept and encourage everyone's vulnerability, beginning with their own, so that truly novel solutions can emerge, nourished by trust and collective intimacy.

## Closing thoughts

Today, design must resist an ideology of performance that only amplifies the problems inherent to the world of business. Through systems thinking, it can support companies in developing the robustness they need to answer the fluctuations of our world. By taking an interest in modes of collaboration, it can answer the dehumanisation of the economic engine. Because in the end, performance and productivity are not objectives in themselves, but outcomes that follow from creating an environment where everyone feels safe and respected.

Would you like to be part of it?
