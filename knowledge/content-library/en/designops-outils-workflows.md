# How DesignOps gives us the keys to building the tools and workflows we actually need
Article written by Arthur Mathon (LinkedIn). Tags: Automation, DesignOps.

## Why do tools and workflows matter?

Tools have always been enablers. Originally they let us hunt game or control fire. Today they help industry find its place in the digital world. They make our team meetings better and more accessible. They cut down laborious, repetitive work so we can focus on the essence of our craft. They standardise production, workflow and exchanges. The benefits are many, but tools are not an end in themselves — they are enablers. In other words, they let teams deliver quality services quickly and offer an optimal experience with a minimum of friction points. Those friction points exist in human/machine, machine/machine and human/human interfaces. Necessary as they are, and sources of information or energy transfer, it is in these meeting zones that errors occur and that part of the content can scatter. Tools are therefore to a team what oil is to an engine: an enabler of transfer as much as a guarantee of long-term operation. In this article we try to answer the following question: how can DesignOps redefine our craft by taking an interest in the way we work together? We will therefore focus on the second pillar of DesignOps described in the image below.

Why should we build systems and tools to help designers do their job? Why, like the proverbial poorly shod cobbler, should designers spend their lives designing innovative products and services for everyone except themselves? We deserve to work in a technical environment we have defined ourselves, whose rules and exchanges we have calibrated. "I find it so ironic that designers spend their lives making product and process experiences better for everyone but themselves." — Jon Fukuda. As we saw in the previous chapter, the less we have to jump from one tool to another, and the more integrated our tools are, the more effective we become as multidisciplinary teams. Consequently, it is not enough to have excellent tools for doing our work; they must also help us communicate with one another and smooth out our workflow. Only one path is open to us: integration! Within your DesignOps team you will therefore need someone able to take charge of integrating these tools, to soften the workflow and reduce the disruption caused by context switching. Larger companies can afford the luxury of more specialised roles, such as Design System Manager, Process Manager or Project Management Office. These people will soon become a shield between your teams' productivity and the interference your tools can generate.

## How do you choose your tools well?

## Methodology

To help you choose, start by discovering how your tools interact with each other at every stage of your process. Map your governance model as well. List the roles and responsibilities of your team members. Conducting these studies will give you an overall picture of your organisation and of the needs of the people within it. This kind of analysis will also help you redefine everyone's roles and permissions across all tools, at every stage of your workflow — which makes licence management far easier. If you struggle to define everyone's role in your new system, the RACI model is a good place to turn. Ask yourself:

- R = who is responsible for the project?
- A = who approves?
- C = who can help or offer an opinion?
- I = who needs to be informed of progress?

## Measuring performance

Flexibility and security are important considerations when you start looking at integrating new tools.

- Flexibility reduces context switching and improves productivity at the same time.
- Security brings peace of mind and trust, both for you and for your clients. This last aspect becomes increasingly important as integration grows: a leak in one tool weakens the security of every other tool connected to it. The last thing to consider is the reliability of the tools you adopt. Reliability is tied to how the tool and its vendor evolve. To assess it, you can look at, for example:

- how frequently new features and updates ship
- how important the product is to the vendor's strategy
- the vendor's financial health in the market
- where the market trend is heading

## DesignOps to steer and safeguard a system that is both integrated and durable

Once you have put your framework, tools and processes in place, you will run into transition difficulties. You will notice that moving from one tool to another is much harder than adding one to your set. Maturity models can help you visualise and track the adoption of your tools at each stage and for each person or team. This kind of model will help you monitor your company's capacity for continuous improvement. You will understand the challenges ahead of you according to where you sit in the maturity model.

## Service Design: the future of DesignOps?

If you start to see your company or organisation as a product, and your design team as Service Design, you will be able to see the expectations and needs of every one of your colleagues. "The future of DesignOps is Service Design." — Dominique Ward

Service design is not only concerned with the experience; it studies HOW that experience is delivered. In that sense service design goes beyond product design. By adopting this philosophy you become able to redefine your workflow so that every tool is fully integrated into your value chain. Tools become solutions shaped directly around the real needs of your users.
