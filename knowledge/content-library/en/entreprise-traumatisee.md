# How do you get a traumatised company moving again?

Article written by Arthur Mathon (version 2, expanded). Tags: DesignOps, OrgDesign, Research.

What I want to explore here is trauma in the world of work — not the trauma of individuals, but that of the organisation itself. My premise is this: we can draw on discoveries in neuroscience to understand the mechanisms of organisational trauma. A company is not made of a neural network, but it does connect individuals who have one. To begin this exploration, let us first define the terms we will be using, all of them borrowed from the living world and from psychology: trauma, resilience and tensegrity.

Applying the work of Boris Cyrulnik, the renowned neuropsychiatrist, to the professional world, a "traumatised company" can be seen as an organisation marked by a shock or a series of crises — brutal restructurings, major revenue losses, strategic failures, excessive pressure, radical strategic shifts imposed from above — that leave it frozen, unable to respond constructively to the challenges in front of it. Just as an individual can be paralysed by trauma, a frozen company locks itself into dysfunctional defence mechanisms that undermine its ability to evolve.

Tensegrity, for its part, describes a company's ability to use internal and external tensions to find new points of balance. Semantically, the word is born of the union of tension and integrity. It rests on a subtle equilibrium between fixed elements, such as the organisation's values or its purpose, and more flexible ones such as processes, teams and culture. By redistributing internal tensions, tensegrity fosters resilience, collaboration and innovation within the organisation.

Finally, a company's resilience is its capacity to overcome crises and disruptions while maintaining its essential activities. Contrary to received wisdom, resilience is not the ability to resist, but rather to adapt structures and processes in response to changing context and the unforeseen. A resilient company nurtures a culture of innovation, encourages constructive challenge and continuous improvement, and supports its people by cultivating an environment of trust and solidarity. Underpinned by this psychological safety, it can act on adapting its mental models (Peter Senge).

## The symptoms of a traumatised company: fear, inertia and resistance to change

According to Boris Cyrulnik, trauma triggers a "freeze" reaction in individuals, in which fear takes over and inhibits creative, adaptive responses. In a traumatised company this translates into a culture of insecurity: fear of failure, fear of innovation, fear of taking risks. Employees and leaders alike may adopt rigid behaviours that curb initiative and build resistance to change. Instead of exploring new possibilities or innovating, the organisation retreats into processes that reassure it but that have become disconnected from its reality. It then reproduces the same patterns, which can lead to total inertia.

## Organisational defence mechanisms: mistrust and closure

A traumatised company may also display defence mechanisms that make it hostile to new ideas or to change. Neuropsychology, through the work of Ruth A. Lanius, describes very well how trauma can create a withdrawal into oneself, where mistrust of the outside — clients, new employees, partners — becomes a kind of protection. In a company this can show up as a closed organisational culture, where any criticism or proposal for change is perceived as a threat. In that setting, production silos become airtight and stop talking to one another. This chronic mistrust prevents open communication and the sharing of ideas, and makes the working climate toxic.

## Loss of meaning and emotional exhaustion

Traumatised individuals often find themselves disconnected from their values and their vital momentum, which damages their motivation and day-to-day engagement. In the same way, a traumatised company can go through a loss of meaning in its activities, its identity and its mission. It shrinks to a set of procedures with no overall vision or purpose, which generates emotional exhaustion among its people. The absence of vision, of purpose, of a shared awareness of what the work does to its ecosystem, or of a common objective, provokes deep disengagement — reducing employees to executors with little involvement in their company's development.

## Being locked into beliefs that deepen the freeze

Cyrulnik speaks of "frozen beliefs" in traumatised people, where negative or self-destructive views of the world crystallise and become inner barriers. In a traumatised company these beliefs translate into a pessimistic view of the future and a self-fulfilling prophecy. The organisation convinces itself that it is destined to fail, and every action reinforces that conviction. It often surfaces in phrases such as "We've always done it this way", "We can't change", or "You'll wear yourself out trying to change things, management doesn't listen to us". These beliefs end up limiting the prospects for innovation and prevent any positive transformation.

## What the freeze does to company culture and viability

A traumatised company can find its very existence at risk over the long term. Unable to adapt to market changes, it loses its competitiveness. Talent may leave, deepening the exhaustion and the stagnation. Rigidity also holds back agility, and the lack of resilience means every difficulty, however minor, is experienced as a major crisis. The inability to bounce back after a failure turns into chronic fragility.

## Breaking free from the freeze: accepting imbalance in order to move forward

The notion that stands opposed to freezing is movement. A human being sets themselves in motion by walking — and walking is nothing other than a succession of controlled imbalances. Each step begins with a slight tip forward, which creates a temporary loss of balance. Rather than correcting it, the body uses it to move ahead. This dynamic process relies on the principles of tensegrity, because the body does not stiffen; it uses its supple structure to absorb and redistribute the forces of movement. The applications are countless, from orthopaedics (Stephen Levin) to architecture with Buckminster Fuller's "Dymaxion House".

Carried over to the world of work, this analogy invites us to question how we apprehend imbalance: instead of perceiving imbalances as threats, an organisation can read them as opportunities to move forward and evolve.

## Active introspection: identifying the root causes of the trauma

The first step towards transformation lies in accepting and acknowledging what is, without judgement and without bias. We can draw on the work of Ralph Waldo Emerson and Colette Poggi who, though separated by time and philosophical tradition, both rely on a millennia-old activity: active introspection. Exploring Indian philosophy, and in particular the concept of māyā, Colette Poggi puts forward the idea that our ordinary perception of the world is veiled by illusions — reducing the "coefficient of illusion" means recognising that what we see is only a reflection limited by our own beliefs.

Applied to a company, active introspection can take the shape of practices such as:

- **Critical reviews of internal processes**: assessing whether organisational practices still match the reality of the market and the needs of the teams — very often, internal processes are merely a replica of habits copied without ever being questioned.
- **Shared vision workshops**: encouraging colleagues to reflect on the company's mission and values in response to crises, in order to recover a collective sense of meaning that is essential to resilience.
- **Reflective leadership**: training leaders in an approach where they openly share their uncertainties and explore new ideas with their teams.

By combining introspection and action, companies learn to see their blockages in order to move past them, drawing on a collective lucidity.

## The path to resilience: acknowledge, accept, change

In his dialogue with Fabrice Midal, Cyrulnik stresses that resilience rests on the capacity to give meaning to ordeals and to find support in order to move beyond trauma. Applied to the professional world, these principles invite a traumatised company to:

- **Acknowledge and analyse the traumas**: mobilise active introspection and the discipline of mental models (Senge) to admit failures, identify their root causes, and learn from mistakes rather than burying them.
- **Establish a culture of support**: create spaces for communication where people can speak without fear of judgement, and promote empathetic leadership.
- **Encourage flexibility and innovation**: replace rigidity with processes that encourage innovation and treat error as a source of learning.
- **Breathe new life into a shared, motivating collective vision**: involve colleagues in creating a purpose — reintroducing meaning, where the individual and the collective feed into work that is greater than either.
- **Rebuild internal and external ties**: encourage connections between departments; support networks are essential to rebuilding trust.
- **Introduce a "resilience mentor"**: far beyond the HR function, a caring and inspiring form of leadership that guides teams through turbulence, offers reference points and fosters a psychologically safe climate.

Setting things in motion again is a long-term undertaking and runs counter to the culture of immediacy — but it can be initiated through concrete actions that are simple and quick.

## Tensegrity: dynamic balance for structural stability

Tensegrity, or integrity through tension, is a structure in which rigid and flexible elements interact to create balance. In the human body, bones are under compression while muscles and tendons act as cables under tension, allowing the body to stand, move and withstand impact. In a company, the "rigid rods" might be the core values, the critical roles or the strict rules; the "cables under tension" might be the culture of innovation, the relationships between teams and individual skills. A company in tensegrity maintains a balance between these rigid and flexible aspects in order to manage tensions and adapt to change — for instance by placing a high value on innovation (tension) while keeping a bedrock of immovable values (compression).

## Redistributing internal tensions: towards greater resilience

As early as 1990, Peter Senge proposed a systemic approach to organisations, in which every element plays an essential role in the balance of the whole. Systems thinking allows us to understand the organisation as an integrated whole — like the elements of a tensegrity structure. Understanding mental models prompts us to question rigid processes; shared vision and team learning keep the organisation in a fluid, adaptive dynamic.

In practice, when certain parts of the organisation are put under tension — during a crisis or a period of high demand — others can absorb some of that pressure by adjusting their priorities. An example: in a consultancy facing a drop in business, the delivery teams (engineers, designers) can support the sales teams — using their domain expertise to target the right companies to approach, taking administrative work off their hands, providing talking points — while using that time to redefine a shared vision and turn the setback into an opportunity to revisit the offer. The benefit: creating enthusiasm that feeds solidarity.

"In organisations, real power and energy is generated through relationships. The patterns of relationships and the capacities to form them are more important than tasks, functions, roles and positions." (Margaret Wheatley)

## Distributing responsibility and authority

In a distributed authority model, tensegrity takes the form of an intelligent spread of responsibilities. Instead of concentrating decisions at the top, companies can decentralise certain functions, allowing teams to self-manage while keeping overall cohesion. This "decision-making tensegrity" eases the pressure on the central hierarchy and distributes tensions to levels where they can be better absorbed — much like holacratic organisations, where each circle holds its own decision-making authority.

## Improving communication to balance tensions between teams

Organisational tensegrity also rests on fluid communication. When communication is transparent, tensions can be redistributed proactively. In a company with ambitious growth targets, regular communication between the product, sales and support teams lets each adjust its pace in light of the others' priorities, avoiding overload while maintaining balance.

## The limits of psychologising the professional world

Psychologising a company the way we do a human being is an approach to be handled with nuance. Some psychological principles genuinely enrich how an organisation works — organisational resilience makes it possible to bounce back from crises by cultivating adaptability and solidarity, and taking well-being seriously contributes to healthier working conditions. But a company remains an organisation with economic objectives, a legal framework and responsibilities towards its stakeholders. Transposing individual psychological concepts too literally risks disconnecting the effort from business and structural objectives, creating unrealistic expectations ("repairing" every employee), or bringing about a culture of over-control at the expense of authenticity — a pressure towards "happiness at work" that becomes just one more norm.

What matters above all is reconnecting the individuals in a system in order to restructure it. A balanced approach runs through collective emotional intelligence and empathetic leadership: acknowledging human needs at work without burdening the company with the impossible mission of "healing" individuals. Psychologising the company in moderation brings benefits in terms of well-being, motivation and collective resilience, while respecting the individuality and complexity of people.

## Looking ahead: towards a resilient organisation inspired by living systems

A truly resilient system integrates tensegrity to spread tensions, uses robustness to maintain its integrity under pressure, and above all accepts the idea of transformation as a condition of survival. It is this harmonious combination of tensegrity, robustness and resilience that forges systems capable of thriving in uncertainty, absorbing external pressures and renewing themselves in the face of challenges. Because in the end, resilience is a combination of vulnerabilities and strengths. Companies, like individuals, can learn not merely to withstand disruption but to draw resources from it in order to evolve — to see every crisis as an opportunity for transformation.
